Provisional tax: the February IRP6, the 80%/90% rule and penalties

Who must pay provisional tax, when the second IRP6 is due, how the 80% and 90% estimate rules work, and the 10% and 20% penalties SARS can charge.

By MF Banda, SARS registered tax practitioner PR-0034776 · Published 23 November 2026

Provisional tax is how people with income that is not a salary pay income tax during the year rather than in one large amount on assessment. It catches many people by surprise: rental income, a side business, freelance work or large interest income can all make you a provisional taxpayer. Missing the deadline, or estimating too low, leads to penalties and interest.

For individuals with a February year-end, the second IRP6 for the 2027 tax year is due by 28 February 2027. That date is a Sunday, so plan to submit and pay by Friday 26 February 2027 at the latest.

Are you a provisional taxpayer?

According to SARS, provisional taxpayers include:

  • individuals who earn income that is not remuneration, such as business, freelance or rental income;
  • companies; and
  • anyone SARS has notified that they are a provisional taxpayer.

An individual with no business income is excluded if either:

  • their taxable income will not exceed the tax threshold; or
  • their interest, dividends, rental and other income not taxed through PAYE will not exceed R30 000 for the year.

The three payments

Payment Due (February year-end) Required?
First period 31 August, six months into the tax year Yes
Second period Last day of February, the end of the tax year Yes
Third ("top-up") After year-end (30 September for individuals) Optional, reduces interest

You must submit the IRP6 even if the amount payable is nil. If the second-period estimate is not submitted, you are treated as having estimated nil taxable income, unless the return is submitted within four months after year-end.

How accurate must the second estimate be?

The second estimate should be close to your actual taxable income for the full year. SARS applies an underestimation penalty of 20% of the shortfall in tax when the estimate is too low:

Actual taxable income When the penalty applies
R1 million or less The estimate is below 90% of actual taxable income and below the "basic amount"
More than R1 million Measured against 80% of actual taxable income

The basic amount is generally your taxable income on your last assessment, excluding taxable capital gains and certain lump sums. It is increased by 8% a year if that assessment is more than 18 months old.

SARS's own example: actual tax of R100 000 with provisional payments of only R60 000 leaves a R40 000 shortfall, and a 20% penalty of R8 000 can be charged on top.

Late payment: 10%

A 10% penalty applies to provisional tax paid late for the first and second periods. Interest is charged on late and underpaid amounts and is not tax-deductible. If the 20% underestimation penalty also applies, it is reduced by the 10% late-payment penalty, so the two are not simply added together.

SARS can remit a penalty if it is satisfied that the delay was not intended to evade or postpone payment. Our guide to penalty remission requests explains how.

Practical steps before February

  1. Update your figures to at least January. Use actual income and expenses for 11 months plus a realistic February.
  2. Include capital gains if you sold property or shares this year.
  3. Remember tax already paid, such as PAYE on a salary and your first provisional payment.
  4. Pay from your bank using the correct payment reference from the IRP6, so the payment is allocated correctly.
  5. Consider a third payment after year-end if your final figures turn out higher.
