Received a SARS letter of demand? Your options explained

Owe SARS and received a letter of demand? The four options SARS allows, what a payment arrangement needs, and what happens if you do nothing.

By MF Banda, SARS registered tax practitioner PR-0034776 · Published 20 November 2026

A letter of demand from SARS means SARS regards an amount as due and unpaid, and that collection steps can follow. The worst response is no response. SARS gives you four ways to deal with the debt, and which one fits depends on whether the amount is correct and what you can afford.

First, check that the amount is right

Before paying or arranging anything, confirm what SARS says you owe and why. You can check your balance on SARS eFiling, the SARS MobiApp, by SMS to 47277, by USSD on *134*7277#, or through the SARS Contact Centre on 0800 00 7277.

Compare the statement of account with your own records. Look for:

  • payments that were made but not allocated to the right tax type or period;
  • estimated or additional assessments you were not aware of;
  • penalties and interest added to the balance;
  • returns that are still outstanding, which can make the balance look higher than the real liability.

If the debt is wrong, the route is a correction, an objection or an account query, not a payment arrangement.

The four options SARS allows

Option When it fits What SARS asks for
Pay in full You can settle the balance Payment of the full balance; SARS's page refers to settling within seven days
Instalment or deferral arrangement (section 167 of the Tax Administration Act) The debt is correct but you cannot pay at once Reasons, proof and a Collection Information Statement (CIS)
Compromise (sections 200 to 202) You cannot realistically pay the full debt A CIS and supporting financial information
Suspension of payment (section 164) You dispute the debt An application while the dispute is pending

What a payment arrangement needs

SARS can agree to deferral or instalments, but only on its criteria. Its guidance says SARS may enter into an arrangement where, for example:

  • you lack assets or cash now, and this is reasonably certain to improve;
  • you expect income that can be used to settle the debt;
  • immediate collection would be poor or uneconomical but is likely to improve;
  • you provide any security SARS requires;
  • all outstanding returns and reconciliations have been submitted.

Two points catch many taxpayers. The arrangement must cover the entire debt, and SARS will only consider it once non-compliance has been remedied. If you defaulted on an earlier arrangement, you must give valid reasons before a new request is considered.

Interest keeps accruing on unpaid debt while you pay it off, and if you break the arrangement's conditions it is terminated and normal collection resumes. SARS can also decline a request.

Making the request on eFiling

For personal income tax, company income tax, trusts, PAYE, UIF, SDL and VAT, the request can be made on eFiling. A Payment Arrangement button appears where there is outstanding debt, including on the statement of account and on final demand letters. Only one tax type can be included per request.

eFiling proposes a plan, which defaults to six months, and you can propose different terms of between 1 and 36 months. Supporting documents may be required; for salaried individuals, SARS lists six months of bank statements, a recent payslip and proof of outstanding accounts.

Once submitted, a request cannot be cancelled, and it is not finalised until any required documents are uploaded. Check the plan carefully before you submit.

If you disagree with the debt

Lodge a request for correction or an objection, depending on the reason. SARS's guidance is clear that you must still pay while a dispute is handled unless a suspension of payment has been granted, and interest continues to accrue. A suspension request under section 164 is a separate application.

What happens if you do nothing

SARS lists the collection steps it can take, including:

  • appointing a third party, such as your bank, employer or customers, to pay over money owed to you;
  • obtaining a civil judgment;
  • attaching and selling assets;
  • holding directors or related parties liable in some circumstances;
  • handing long-outstanding debt to external debt collectors, after notifying you.

Acting early keeps more options open: an arrangement or a compromise is considered before enforcement, not after.
